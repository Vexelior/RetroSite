Anatomy of a 90s Homepage
A field guide to the things every personal page had

The Hit Counter

Before analytics dashboards, the only way to know if anyone was reading your page was a hit counter: a little odometer of digit images, usually green-on-black or LED-red. It ran on a server script (often a Perl CGI) or a free counter service that served you the image. Most counters went up every time the page loaded, so webmasters would quietly hit Reload a few dozen times. (Ours counts your visits on top of a respectable starting number. We would never.)

"Under Construction"

The yellow sign, the digging stick figure, the hazard stripes. Pages were never "done," and everybody wanted you to know a cool new section was coming soon. When the Internet Archive went through its saved copy of GeoCities, it found hundreds of different under-construction GIFs, and artist Jason Eppink later put them in an exhibit at the Museum of the Moving Image. The ones on this site were drawn new for it, with the same yellow-and-black look.

The Guestbook

The comments section before comment sections. Visitors typed their name, e-mail, homepage URL, and how they found you, then left a note ("cool site!!! check out mine!"). It was usually powered by a borrowed CGI script or a free hosted service, and it was often flooded with spam by the end of the decade. Sign ours!

Webrings

Search engines were bad, so fans linked their sites into rings. At the bottom of each page was a little box with Previous, Next, and Random links, and clicking them would take you around a circle of sites about the same topic. The idea started in 1994, and in 1995 Sage Weil turned his ring script into a company called WebRing.

<BLINK> and <MARQUEE>

The two most famous tags of the Browser Wars. Netscape had <BLINK>: like this! Microsoft answered with <MARQUEE>, which scrolled text across the screen: <<< Hello from 1995! This text is scrolling with a real MARQUEE tag! >>> BOING! BOING! BEHAVIOR=ALTERNATE! Neither tag ever became part of an official HTML standard. Today's browsers still support <MARQUEE> for old pages, but <BLINK> is gone; the blinking on this site is faked with a few lines of CSS.

Animated GIFs


Spinning globes, spinning e-mail envelopes, flashing NEW! badges, flaming horizontal rules. GIF animation took off after Netscape 2.0 began looping them in 1996. Free "clip art" sites gave away thousands, and people collected them like stickers. The Internet Archive's GifCities search engine has millions of them saved from GeoCities.

Tiled Backgrounds

Outer space Olde parchment Plain browser gray Hacker black
Netscape 1.1 let you tile any image behind your page with <BODY BACKGROUND="...">. Popular picks were starfields, marble, parchment, clouds, and bright patterns that made text almost impossible to read. Netscape's own documentation warned about this. This page uses the "left stripe" trick: a background image 1280 pixels wide, so it only repeats downward and draws a colored sidebar.

Table Layouts & the Spacer GIF

There was no CSS layout, so designers used <TABLE> (meant for data) to place everything on the page. To force exact gaps, they used a transparent 1x1 pixel GIF stretched to any size with WIDTH and HEIGHT. Frames

Netscape 2.0 (1996) let one window show several HTML files at once using <FRAMESET>. That meant you could keep a navigation menu on the left without copying it onto every page. The downside: bookmarks and the Back button broke, search engines got lost, and other sites could "frame" your page inside theirs. That's why so many sites had a "Frames / No Frames" choice on the front page, just like ours. Frames are not supported in HTML5, but browsers still display them.

Background MIDI Music

MIDI files were tiny (a whole song in a few kilobytes) and perfect for dial-up. You dropped one in with <EMBED SRC="song.mid" AUTOSTART=TRUE HIDDEN=TRUE> (Netscape) or <BGSOUND SRC="song.mid" LOOP=INFINITE> (Internet Explorer), and your visitors' sound cards started playing it right away. Nobody could find the "stop" button. Modern browsers block auto-playing sound and no longer play MIDI, so our theme song uses a click-to-play stand-in. (Download the real theme.mid.)

"Best Viewed With..."



 Netscape and Internet Explorer each had their own tags and bugs, so pages often only looked right in one of them. Webmasters put up little 88x31 pixel buttons saying which browser and screen size to use: "Best viewed with Netscape Navigator at 800x600." Some web designers fought back with the Any Browser campaign, which said pages should work for everybody. That idea won in the end and became the web standards movement.
